An owner app that puts the rider’s bike first

Connecting a phone to a bike is the first thing a rider does, and it is where the competitor apps we studied failed most. Bajaj’s prototype worked but felt dated, so I led the redesign of Mera Bajaj. It has been live on the App Store and Google Play for about two years, with over 100,000 downloads.

The short version

Turning Bajaj’s prototype owner app into a premium Pulsar app.

  • Problem The app worked, but the screens were plain, the flows inconsistent, and it didn’t feel like a premium motorcycle brand.
  • Brief Make it modern and polished, with smooth interactions and an upmarket feel, before it reached owners.
  • Outcome Live on the App Store and Google Play, with 100,000+ downloads.

My part I led UX and mentored a junior designer, from research and a competitor study to hi-fi screens, a style guide and a component library.

What made it hard

  • Bluetooth is where the competitor apps fail. In our teardown, pairing often failed, took too long or dropped, and trip data wasn’t recorded.
  • Feature-rich apps were hard to navigate. The competitors had over-complicated navigation and too much data crammed into a few modules.
  • Errors and permissions left riders guessing. Error messages weren’t informative, and some apps captured the wrong location.
  • Bajaj’s brand rules set the palette. That limited how far the visual design could go, and I worked inside them.

How my thinking changed along the way

Four tabs, each with one job

Research, personas, user stories and app-store reviews all pointed to the same few daily jobs: check the bike, plan a route, look back at a trip, book a service. So the bottom bar became Home, Navigate, Trips and Services, and everything else sits in one secondary menu: reminders, community, riding tips, FAQs and settings. An early version had a fifth tab for Music. I cut it to keep the bar to the four jobs riders named. The cost is that less-used features are one level deeper.

The bike became the home screen

Connecting the bike is the first thing a rider does, and the step competitors handled worst. The user story was clear: connect “in a single step, the first time”, then pair automatically on every ride. So Home opens on the owner’s bike, with photo, model, registration and a Bluetooth button on the card, followed by upcoming service and recent trips. The trade-off is that only one bike is on screen at a time. Owners with more than one switch by tapping the bike name at the top, and the connection screen lists every bike with its status and a “Connect Another Bike” button.

Permissions needed a reason

Riders refuse permissions they don’t understand, and without location and Bluetooth the app can’t do its main job. So each prompt says what it is for. For location, it says it shows where you are on the map and powers directions, nearby search and travel-time estimates. That means more words on screen at the moment people want to get going, and I accepted it.

The failures needed designing too

Competitor reviews were full of pairing that failed silently, so telling riders what went wrong, and what to do next, mattered as much as the success screen. Bluetooth pairing, navigation and adding a vehicle each have a designed “connection failed” screen, next to the searching, found and connected states. The search screen heads off the most common failure with one line: “Make sure your bike’s ignition is switched on”. It meant more screens to design and hand off for every flow.

From user stories to task flows

I turned the research and app-store reviews into user stories for each persona. Each story then became a task flow with the fewest steps that still collected what Bajaj needed.

What we cut, and what we tried

Each persona got a moodboard, and I put the prototype Bajaj gave us next to the competitor teardown to see what to keep and what to drop. From there it went through low-fidelity wireframes and the information architecture, with light and dark mode flows alongside. The fifth tab was the clearest cut, and the “From wireframe to final” pairs below show it. Less-used features moved into the secondary menu.

Four visual directions, one brand

We explored widely before settling. From the persona moodboards I built four visual routes, Solid, Dark, Glassmorphic and Gradient, and tried each on the same home screen. Then Bajaj’s brand rules decided the final palette: Bajaj blue, in light and dark. The iterations below show the home screen moving from the four routes toward that blue.

What happened after launch

  • 100,000+ downloads of Mera Bajaj, live for about two years on the App Store and Google Play for Pulsar owners.
  • I delivered hi-fi designs in light and dark themes, a style guide and a component library for development.
  • Followed by the Chetak EV display, and later Bajaj’s 5” TFT cluster, whose menu system now ships on the Pulsar N160 SS.

What didn’t go to plan

There was no formal usability testing. Bajaj ran none on the design, so I can’t say how riders found it beyond the download numbers.

Bajaj’s brand rules limited how far the visual design could go, and I worked within them.

What I’d do next

I would push the UI further. With what I know now, I would make a stronger case for a more distinctive look inside Bajaj’s brand rules.
