The problem

Bajaj’s owner app worked as a prototype but didn’t feel like a premium motorcycle brand. It covered after-sales and riding: profile, vehicles, trips, service bookings and FAQs, but the screens were plain and the flows inconsistent.

The brief was to turn it into a modern, polished app with smooth interactions and an upmarket feel, before it reached owners.

My role

  • Led a two-person team from kick-off to asset delivery, mentoring a newly joined junior designer.
  • Ran user research and read reviews of Bajaj’s existing apps to understand riders’ day-to-day use.
  • Ran a competitor study of four motorcycle apps, including KTM, covering visual design and app flows.
  • Built proto-personas and user stories for trips and riding.
  • Designed the information architecture, low-fidelity wireframes and visual direction.
  • Delivered hi-fi screens in light and dark modes, a style guide and a component library.

What we learned

Bluetooth is where competitor apps fail.

In the teardown, pairing often failed, took too long or dropped, and trip data wasn’t recorded.

→

Put the bike and its connection status at the top of the home screen, one tap to connect.

Feature-rich apps were hard to navigate.

Competitors had over-complicated navigation and too much data crammed into a few modules.

→

A small set of top-level destinations, each with one job.

Errors and permissions left riders guessing.

Error messages weren’t informative, and some apps captured the wrong location.

→

Explain every permission in plain words before asking for it.

Key decisions

Decision 1

Four tabs, each with one job

Chose
A bottom bar with Home, Navigate, Trips and Services. Everything else, such as reminders, community, riding tips, FAQs and settings, sits in one secondary menu. An early version had a fifth tab for Music; it was cut to keep the bar to the four jobs riders named.
Why
User research, personas, user stories and app-store reviews all pointed to the same few daily jobs: check the bike, plan a route, look back at a trip, book a service.
Trade-off
Less-used features are one level deeper.
Decision 2

The bike is the home screen

Chose
Home opens on the owner’s bike: photo, model and registration, with a Bluetooth button on the card, followed by upcoming service and recent trips.
Why
Connecting the bike is the first thing a rider does, and the step competitors handled worst. The user story was clear: connect “in a single step, the first time”, then pair automatically on every ride.
Trade-off
Only one bike is on screen at a time. Owners with more than one switch by tapping the bike name at the top, marked with a down arrow. The connection screen lists every bike with its status and a “Connect Another Bike” button.
Decision 3

Ask for permissions with a reason

Chose
Each permission prompt says what it is for. For location: it shows where you are on the map and powers directions, nearby search and travel-time estimates.
Why
Riders refuse permissions they don’t understand, and without location and Bluetooth the app can’t do its main job.
Trade-off
More words on screen at the moment users want to get going.
Decision 4

Design the failures, not just the happy path

Chose
Bluetooth pairing, navigation and adding a vehicle each have a designed “connection failed” screen, alongside searching, found and connected states. The search screen also prevents the most common failure with one line: “Make sure your bike’s ignition is switched on”.
Why
Competitor reviews were full of pairing that failed silently. Telling riders what went wrong, and what to do next, was as important as the success screen.
Trade-off
More screens to design and hand off for every flow.

Four visual directions, one brand

We explored widely before settling. Each persona got a moodboard, and from those we built four visual routes, Solid, Dark, Glassmorphic and Gradient, each tried on the same home screen. Bajaj’s brand rules then set the final palette: Bajaj blue, in light and dark.

Validation and impact

  • Live for about two years as Mera Bajaj on the App Store and Google Play, for Pulsar owners.
  • Delivered hi-fi designs in light and dark themes, a style guide and a component library for development.
  • Bajaj ran no formal usability testing on the design.
  • Followed by the Chetak EV display, and later Bajaj’s 5” TFT cluster, whose menu system now ships on the Pulsar N160 SS.

Reflection

I would push the UI further. Bajaj’s brand rules limited how far the visual design could go, and I worked within them. With what I know now, I would make a stronger case for a more distinctive UI inside those rules.
