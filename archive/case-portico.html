An HR suite where every role finishes its daily tasks in fewer steps

Portico had every HR and payroll feature a company needs, added one at a time over years. I led a team of four designers to restructure it into one consistent web product and a focused mobile app.

The problem

Portico worked, but every task took too long. It started as a way to digitise manual HR processes: document archive, payroll, expenses, recruitment, eLearning and payments. New features were added as needed, so each module looked and behaved differently, and common tasks sat several levels deep.

Employees, managers, HR and admins used the same screens for very different jobs. The mobile app tried to copy the web. Portico’s management wanted a mobile-first redesign, delivered in two phases, that would cut support queries in half.

My role

  • Led a team of 4 designers from research to final web and mobile designs.
  • Worked with Portico’s product owner and developers on scope and feasibility, and with end users for testing.
  • Ran the stakeholder alignment workshop remotely on Mural during Covid-19, to agree goals, user segments and success measures.
  • Led an expert review of the existing app and mapped existing flows to count the steps in each key task.
  • Restructured the information architecture through card sorting, and owned the design system for web and mobile.

What we learned

The menu followed the order features were built.

Flow mapping showed common tasks buried several levels deep, with no search and no in-app guidance.

→

Regroup everything into clear categories, add global search, and bring frequent tasks to the front.

Every screen looked different.

The expert review found inconsistent layouts, icons and button weights, low contrast and a loader on every action.

→

One design system across all modules, with one clear primary action per screen.

Each role does a few things every day.

Employees clock in, apply for leave and download payslips. Managers approve requests and check their team. HR and admins manage people, documents and company data.

→

A default home for each role, built from its daily tasks.

Key decisions

Decision 1

A default home for each role, which users can customise

Options
One shared dashboard for everyone, a fixed home per role, or role-based defaults that users can change.
Chose
Separate defaults for employees, managers, HR and admins, based on each role’s daily activities. Anyone can pin their own shortcuts to Favourites.
Why
A shared dashboard would be cluttered for everyone. A fixed one would fail people whose work doesn’t match their title.
Trade-off
Four home layouts to design, test and maintain instead of one.
Decision 2

Mobile does the quick tasks, not everything the web does

Options
Port the full web product to mobile, or design mobile as its own product for tasks people do on the move.
Chose
A task-focused app: clock in, apply for leave or work from home, approve requests, view payslips and sign documents. Data-heavy areas such as document management, HR management and company administration stay on the web.
Why
Those areas carry too much data to work on a phone, and nobody needs them in a hurry. Quick tasks are what people open the app for.
Trade-off
Some users can’t do everything from their phone, and have to switch to the web for heavier work.
Decision 3

Rebuild the navigation around categories users recognise

Chose
Card sorting produced clear top-level categories: Leaves & Attendance, Payroll, Learning & Development, Documents, Reports and Company. Global search on the web covers everything, for example “Leaves, Payroll”.
Why
The old structure matched how the product was built, not how people think about their work.
Trade-off
It meant significant rework for the development team, which later limited how much of the redesign shipped (see Reflection).

The solution

Validation and impact

  • 30% fewer steps across core admin tasks after the navigation was regrouped into clear categories.
  • Applying for leave: 1 tap from the home screen’s frequently used tasks, against 4 through the full menu (All → Leaves & Attendance → Leaves → +).
  • Approving a request: 1 tap from the notification, with no need to open the request.
  • Early user testing showed the new navigation helped people find information and complete tasks.
  • Post-launch figures, such as support queries, were not shared with us after the engagement ended.

Reflection

I should have pushed harder for users earlier. Near the end of our engagement, Portico’s product owner left the company, and the decision on what to build passed to the developers. Facing a deadline, they chose to skip changes that meant rework, and some of the best improvements didn’t ship.

What I did: I agreed a smaller scope that met their deadline, and handed over a prioritised backlog of the remaining UX improvements for them to pick up after launch. Next time I would set up that phased plan at the start, so improvements are scheduled from the beginning, not traded away at the end.
